## Primary research question

> Which responsibilities distinguish software engineering from programming, and where does the distinction affect outcomes?

## Supporting questions

1. How have academic, professional, standards, and practitioner sources defined software engineering and programming?
2. Which activities and responsibilities are consistently associated with software engineering beyond producing source code?
3. Where do the concepts overlap, and under which definitions does programming include broader engineering work?
4. Which distinctions depend on project scale, lifecycle stage, organizational setting, system criticality, or professional role?
5. What empirical evidence connects the identified responsibilities—or their absence—to software, project, team, or user outcomes?
6. Which common claims about the distinction are unsupported, overstated, or primarily rhetorical?

## Why the question matters

The book aims to investigate what trustworthy participation in software engineering requires. It cannot evaluate an AI engineering system until it establishes which work belongs to the subject and which distinctions are consequential.

If software engineering is treated as synonymous with code production without evidence, later chapters may omit responsibilities involving problem framing, design, coordination, verification, operation, maintenance, risk, and system evolution. If the distinction is exaggerated, the book may create artificial boundaries or treat occupational labels as evidence of capability.

The chapter must therefore establish a bounded relationship supported by definitions, observations of work, and evidence about outcomes. Its purpose is understanding, not defending the status of a profession or proving the book's starting belief.

## Expected contribution

The chapter is expected to produce:

- a map of major definitions and their contexts;
- a taxonomy of responsibilities attributed to programming, software engineering, or both;
- an account of where the boundary is clear, overlapping, or dependent on context;
- an evidence map connecting responsibilities to reported outcomes;
- a distinction between conceptual claims and empirically supported consequences;
- confidence-rated findings; and
- unresolved questions needed by later chapters.

These are expected forms of output, not expected conclusions.

## Included scope

The investigation includes:

- software development and engineering activities across the lifecycle;
- academic and professional definitions;
- software-engineering standards and recognized bodies of knowledge;
- empirical studies of developer and software-engineer work;
- historical sources when they explain how the distinction developed;
- practitioner evidence describing responsibilities and consequences in context;
- programming as implementation and as a potentially broader practice, depending on the source's definition;
- individual and collective responsibilities; and
- reported outcomes affecting software, projects, teams, users, operations, or maintenance.

## Excluded scope

The investigation excludes:

- legal regulation or licensure of the title “engineer,” except where it materially affects a definition used in the evidence;
- salary, prestige, hiring-title, and career-ladder comparisons that do not clarify work or outcomes;
- arguments that define one role as superior without inspectable evidence;
- detailed evaluation of AI coding or engineering systems, which belongs to Part IV;
- decisions about Zelyq features or architecture;
- a comprehensive history of programming languages or software engineering; and
- general systems, computer, electrical, or other engineering disciplines except where directly relevant to the software distinction.

The proposal does not fix final definitions. The research will record how sources use these terms and will state any operational definition adopted for synthesis.

## Proposed research mode

The chapter will combine:

1. **Structured evidence synthesis** to locate and compare definitions, responsibility models, empirical studies of work, and evidence about outcomes.
2. **Conceptual analysis** to distinguish claims that use the same terms differently and to construct a bounded taxonomy without treating the taxonomy as empirical proof.

The chapter will not claim to be a full systematic review of every software-engineering publication. Its search, selection, and coverage claims will be limited to the protocol actually executed.

## Expected evidence

Relevant evidence may include:

- standards and recognized bodies of knowledge;
- peer-reviewed empirical studies of software-development work;
- systematic and mapping reviews;
- occupational and task studies;
- historical primary sources;
- software-engineering education and competency frameworks;
- engineering handbooks and mature practitioner studies;
- project or incident evidence connecting omitted responsibilities to outcomes; and
- credible arguments challenging the usefulness of the distinction.

Source role will not be confused with evidentiary strength. Each material claim will be assessed under the evidence standard.

## Known risks and biases

### Confirmation bias

The book begins from a concern that code generation is narrower than software engineering. This creates a risk of selecting definitions and examples that support a distinction while ignoring broader accounts of programming or evidence that boundaries are fluid.

Mitigation: include explicit searches for critiques, overlapping definitions, contrary frameworks, and evidence that programming work includes wider responsibilities.

### Occupational-status bias

Sources may use titles to imply hierarchy, professionalism, or competence without demonstrating differences in work.

Mitigation: classify observable activities, responsibilities, contexts, and outcomes separately from job titles.

### Academic and geographic coverage bias

English-language and indexed academic sources may overrepresent particular countries, large organizations, or formal processes.

Mitigation: record coverage limits, seek practitioner and open-source evidence, and avoid generalizing beyond represented settings.

### Survivorship and publication bias

Published success and failure accounts may not represent ordinary work, and organizations may disclose only selected incidents.

Mitigation: treat such sources as context-bound, search for null or conflicting results, and avoid prevalence claims without appropriate sampling.

### Historical presentism

Current terminology may be projected onto earlier sources whose context and meaning differed.

Mitigation: interpret historical material within its period and avoid using chronology alone as proof of conceptual progress.

## Ethical, privacy, security, and licensing considerations

The initial research uses published or lawfully accessible sources and does not recruit human participants or collect private data.

Public developer discussions will not be quoted or analyzed as participant data without a separate ethical assessment. Copyrighted sources will be cited and paraphrased within lawful limits. Research artifacts will not reproduce restricted full text.

## Resources and access required

The research requires access to authoritative standards, scholarly indexes or accessible publisher search, stable source copies, bibliographic metadata, and archives for historical material.

Paywalled evidence will be recorded as inaccessible when full inspection is not possible. Abstracts and search snippets will not be treated as adequate support for substantive claims.

## Proposal decisions required before research

Reviewers should determine:

- whether the primary question is sufficiently bounded;
- whether consequences and outcomes can be assessed without expanding the chapter excessively;
- whether structured evidence synthesis plus conceptual analysis fits the question;
- whether the inclusion and exclusion boundaries preserve relevant contrary perspectives;
- whether the proposed outputs overlap improperly with later chapters; and
- which reviewer expertise is required.

Approval of this proposal would authorize protocol execution and evidence collection. It would not endorse a distinction between software engineering and programming.
