## ERB-01-01-S003

Source: ACM/IEEE-CS Joint Task Force on Computing Curricula, *Software Engineering 2014: Curriculum Guidelines for Undergraduate Degree Programs in Software Engineering*, 23 February 2015.

Stable location: [official IEEE Computer Society PDF](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/reports/SoftwareEngineering_UnderGrad_2014.pdf)

Source role and type: normative undergraduate curriculum and competency framework.

Population, system, and setting: recommended undergraduate software-engineering education; not a sampled practitioner population. Revision input included 477 survey responses from educators and practitioners in 42 countries, conference workshops, public feedback, and expert review.

Relevant content inspected: development process; Chapter 2 on the discipline; expected student outcomes; guiding principles; Software Engineering Education Knowledge structure; professional practice; and discussion of neighboring computing disciplines.

Definition and scope extracted:

- The framework describes software engineering as more than programming and identifies quality, schedule, and economic goals as additional concerns.
- It treats design and process management as central while recognizing implementation, verification, documentation, maintenance, teamwork, communication, coordination, stakeholder work, ethics, legal concerns, and professional standards.
- It warns that the occupational title “software engineer” does not consistently identify a software-engineering professional.
- It acknowledges that precise boundaries between computing disciplines are difficult to draw.

Competencies identified: problem identification and analysis, design, development, implementation, verification, documentation, process evaluation, teamwork, delivery, stakeholder negotiation and communication, professional conduct, economics, societal needs, and context-sensitive selection of approaches.

Level of analysis: discipline and undergraduate educational program.

Method or argumentative basis: joint professional curriculum-development process building on SE2004 and other bodies of knowledge, informed by stakeholder survey responses, workshops, public review, expert review, and task-force judgment.

Relevant propositions:

- Programming techniques effective for individuals or small teams may not scale to large, complex systems.
- Software-engineering work requires tradeoffs among quality, schedule, economic, stakeholder, ethical, and technical concerns.
- Software processes and practices must be adapted to context rather than reduced to one universal lifecycle model.
- Competence includes evaluating both the process followed and the solution produced.

Limitations stated or identified:

- The framework prescribes educational outcomes rather than observing workplace behavior.
- Its survey and consultation informed revisions but do not turn each recommendation into an empirically estimated consensus.
- It focuses on undergraduate preparation and does not represent all knowledge acquired through professional practice.
- The 2015 publication date limits its coverage of later practice and technology.
- Its professionalization and educational purposes may influence how disciplinary boundaries are framed.

Reviewer appraisal:

- Relevance: high for proposed responsibility and competency scope.
- Methodological fit: appropriate for normative educational claims; inappropriate for prevalence or causal outcome claims.
- Directness: direct for ACM/IEEE curriculum recommendations; indirect for actual role boundaries and project outcomes.
- Risk of bias: institutional framing, curriculum-design priorities, consultation-selection effects, and age.

Permitted use in synthesis: support a bounded account of the competencies and concerns that a major joint curriculum framework assigns to software-engineering education; qualify title-based distinctions.

Prohibited inference: do not claim that every software engineer has these competencies, that programmers lack them, that completing such a curriculum guarantees competent practice, or that the framework proves outcome effects.

Candidate claim: in this educational framework, the distinction is responsibility-based rather than title-based: programming and implementation remain necessary, while engineering preparation also includes problem analysis, design, verification, coordination, stakeholder work, professional judgment, and evaluation under constraints.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending.

## ERB-01-01-S001

Source: Hironori Washizaki, ed., *Guide to the Software Engineering Body of Knowledge*, v4.0a, IEEE Computer Society, August 2026.

Stable location: [official IEEE Computer Society PDF](https://ieeecs-media.computer.org/media/education/swebok/swebok-v4.pdf)

Source role and type: professional body of knowledge; normative and consensus framework.

Population, system, and setting: the software-engineering discipline as characterized by the IEEE Computer Society; not a sampled practitioner population.

Relevant content inspected: introduction, pp. xxxvii–xxxix; knowledge-area scope; guide-development and approval material.

Definition and scope extracted:

- Software engineering is characterized as a systematic, disciplined, and quantifiable engineering approach covering development, operation, and maintenance.
- This definition is quoted and attributed by SWEBOK to the SEVOCAB source. ISO/IEC/IEEE 24765 was not independently inspected for this review, so any use of the definition is second-hand through S001 rather than direct evidence from S002.
- Engineering is presented as applying scientific knowledge and practice to real-world problems under resource and cost constraints.
- The guide separates software construction from requirements, architecture, design, testing, operations, maintenance, configuration management, management, process, methods, quality, security, professional practice, economics, and foundations.

Activities and responsibilities identified: requirements, architecture, design, construction, testing, operations, maintenance, configuration management, management, process, models and methods, quality, security, professional practice, economics, and relevant foundations.

Level of analysis: discipline and professional body of knowledge.

Method or argumentative basis: expert-edited, multi-stage professional consensus and review; the guide reports participation by more than 130 reviewers and formal IEEE Computer Society approval.

Reviewer appraisal:

- Relevance: high for definitions and responsibility scope.
- Methodological fit: appropriate for normative and consensus claims; inappropriate as direct evidence of actual work prevalence or causal effects.
- Transparency: editorial structure, objectives, approvals, knowledge areas, and references are documented; consensus-source selection and synthesis are not equivalent to a systematic empirical review.
- Risk of bias: institutional framing and professionalization interests may influence boundaries.
- Directness: direct for the IEEE framework; indirect for the primary question's outcome component.
- Recency: current release, while incorporating historical definitions and prior editions.

Permitted use in synthesis: support claims about how a major professional body defines and scopes software engineering; identify responsibility domains for later empirical testing.

Prohibited inference: do not infer that every software engineer performs all listed activities, that programmers never perform them, or that the framework itself proves outcome effects.

Candidate claims:

- In the IEEE framework, software engineering extends beyond software construction across the software lifecycle and includes technical, managerial, quality, operational, professional, and economic responsibilities.
- Under this framework, programming or construction is one responsibility domain within a broader engineering scope, not a complete synonym for that scope.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending.

## ERB-01-01-S004

Source: Peter Naur and Brian Randell, eds., *Software Engineering: Report of a Conference Sponsored by the NATO Science Committee, Garmisch, Germany, 7th to 11th October 1968*, Scientific Affairs Division, NATO, January 1969.

Stable locations: [Newcastle University repository record](https://eprints.ncl.ac.uk/158767); [inspectable full report](https://navegapolis.com/website/wp-content/uploads/nato1968e.pdf)

Source role and type: primary historical conference report combining working papers with edited, attributed discussion.

Population, system, and setting: more than 50 invited participants from 11 countries, drawn from computer manufacturers, universities, software houses, users, and other groups concerned with software problems. Participants spoke as individuals rather than as organizational representatives.

Relevant content inspected: highlights, preface, conference background, design, production, internal communication, documentation, quality assurance, distribution and updating, field reporting and maintenance, and selected working papers.

Definition and scope extracted:

- The NATO study group deliberately selected “software engineering” as a provocative phrase implying that software manufacture needed theoretical foundations and practical disciplines associated with established engineering fields.
- The conference was organized around software design, production, and service; the report also describes implementation, delivery, distribution, maintenance, management, communication, documentation, testing, and quality assurance.
- Participants treated scale as more than code volume, including the number of people, configurations, operating conditions, changes, and interfaces involved.

Level of analysis: field, organization, project, system, and practitioner argument; no statistically sampled population.

Method or argumentative basis: editors selected and organized passages from submitted papers, recorded discussions, and working-group material. They sought to retain controversy and explicitly did not attempt to establish consensus or a majority position.

Relevant propositions and observations:

- The founding use of the term concerned disciplined production and service across a software system's life, not a new occupational title based only on coding skill.
- Large-system work raised coordination, communication, progress assessment, documentation, testing, configuration, distribution, updating, and maintenance problems alongside implementation.
- The report records disagreement about team size, management formality, design method, and appropriate production controls; it therefore resists a single universal process model.
- Several working materials distinguish a nominal milestone or availability demonstration from substantive quality assurance and continuing field feedback.

Limitations stated or identified:

- This is a curated conference report, not a verbatim transcript, systematic review, representative survey, or controlled study.
- Participant remarks express individual views and experiences; the editors intentionally left major disagreements unresolved.
- The evidence reflects 1968 technologies, institutions, terminology, and concerns.
- The inspected searchable edition was scanned, processed by optical character recognition, and reformatted; its editor warns that transcription errors may remain.
- Historical prominence does not establish current prevalence or causal effects.

Reviewer appraisal:

- Relevance: high for the origin and early intended scope of software engineering.
- Methodological fit: strong as a primary historical record and source of attributed arguments; weak for prevalence, consensus, or outcome-effect claims.
- Directness: direct for what the conference organizers and participants discussed; indirect for contemporary practice and the modern boundary between roles.
- Risk of bias: participant selection, editorial selection, retrospective interpretation by readers, dated context, and OCR error.

Permitted use in synthesis: establish the historical problem framing and show that software engineering was introduced around disciplined, large-system, lifecycle work broader than implementation alone; present disputed propositions as disputed and attributed.

Prohibited inference: do not claim that the conference established a universal definition, that all participants agreed, that its practices caused better outcomes, or that its occupational language maps directly onto modern job titles.

Candidate claim: historically, the proposed distinction was principally about the scope and discipline required to design, produce, deliver, and sustain consequential software systems—not about excluding programming from engineering or assigning permanent boundaries to people.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending.

## ERB-01-01-S008

Source: Márcio Kuroki Gonçalves, Cleidson R. B. de Souza, and Víctor M. González, “Collaboration, Information Seeking and Communication: An Observational Study of Software Developers' Work Practices,” *Journal of Universal Computer Science*, 17(14), 2011, pp. 1913–1930, doi:10.3217/jucs-017-14-1913.

Stable locations: [journal record](https://www.jucs.org/jucs_17_14/collaboration_information_seeking_and.html); [full paper](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/8987/f0686dd01ec97dffdfbe9d3c51c719b1b251.pdf)

Source role and type: in-situ mixed quantitative and qualitative observational study.

Population, system, and setting: 14 developers from two collocated sub-teams in a CMMI level 2 Brazilian software organization; the wider team served a government customer and used a process based on RUP and PMBoK.

Method or argumentative basis: approximately 51 hours of non-participant observation across one to three work shifts per developer, with interviews before and after observations; activities, durations, tools, and interactions were recorded and classified.

Relevant results:

- Collaborative activity occupied approximately 45% of observed time, and information seeking approximately 31.9%.
- Individual and collaborative activities were interwoven; developers sought implementation, business-rule, status, dependency, and colleague-activity information.
- The observed developers were interrupted frequently, with an average uninterrupted interval of approximately 3.07 minutes in this setting.
- The study's role description assigned the 37 developers responsibility for requirements, development, testing, and deployment, demonstrating that its “developer” population was not limited to code entry.

Limitations stated or identified: one organization, 14 participants, about 51 observation hours, collocation, one principal observer, context-sensitive classifications, possible observation effects, and no measured product outcome or title-group comparison.

Permitted use in synthesis: demonstrate that, in the studied setting, development work visibly included substantial collaboration and information work alongside individual implementation activity.

Prohibited inference: do not generalize the reported percentages to all developers, claim that collaboration time caused better outcomes, or use the source to establish a programmer–engineer title boundary.

Candidate claim: responsibility for producing software can require continuous information seeking, coordination, and dependency management that is not visible in source-code output alone.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending.

## ERB-01-01-S009

Source: Gina Venolia, Robert DeLine, and Thomas LaToza, *Software Development at Microsoft Observed: It's About People … Working Together*, Microsoft Research Technical Report MSR-TR-2005-140, October 2005.

Stable locations: [Microsoft Research record](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/software-development-at-microsoft-observed/); [full report](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr-2005-140.pdf)

Source role and type: organizational technical report combining two surveys and semi-structured interviews.

Population, system, and setting: Microsoft architects, software developers, software test developers, development leads, and developers in 2005. The first survey received 157 responses, with 104 developer responses used in the report; 6 development leads and 5 developers were interviewed; the follow-up survey received 187 responses.

Method or argumentative basis: two large questionnaires sent to randomly selected employees by job title, 11 approximately one-hour interviews, transcription of interview notes into about 800 cards, and thematic card sorting.

Relevant results:

- Understanding code rationale, recovering from externally requested task switching, and tracking changes elsewhere were prominent reported problems.
- Developers described maintaining mental models covering architecture, implementation, ownership, history, and planned work, often supplemented through social networks.
- The study reports coordination costs around ownership boundaries, cross-team dependencies, onboarding, and interruption of experienced team members.
- Interview and survey evidence conflicted on the use and maintenance of design documents, and the authors did not resolve the discrepancy.

Limitations stated or identified: one company, 2005 context, limited response rates, self-report and recall bias, selected summary reporting, incomplete instrument and dataset disclosure, no external outcome measures, and no programmer–engineer comparison.

Permitted use in synthesis: bounded evidence that code comprehension and modification depend on rationale, organizational knowledge, coordination, and maintained mental models in the studied organization.

Prohibited inference: do not generalize Microsoft practices to all organizations, equate reported difficulty with measured productivity loss, or infer title-based differences.

Candidate claim: engineering responsibility includes preserving and communicating the rationale and dependency knowledge required for other people to understand, change, and sustain a system.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending.

## ERB-01-01-S014

Source: Yahya Rafique and Vojislav B. Mišić, “The Effects of Test-Driven Development on External Quality and Productivity: A Meta-Analysis,” *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering*, 39(6), 2013, pp. 835–856, doi:10.1109/TSE.2012.28.

Stable location: [accessible full paper](https://raidoninc.com/assets/research/tddMetaAnalysis.pdf)

Source role and type: systematic review and meta-analysis of comparative empirical studies.

Population, system, and setting: 27 studies published through February 2011, spanning academic and industrial settings, varied participant experience, tasks, languages, control processes, and test effort.

Responsibility examined: test-driven development, combining test-first implementation and refactoring; this is one testing and construction technique, not testing as a whole.

Outcomes examined: external quality measured through defects or passed tests and productivity measured through time, output, or effort metrics.

Method or argumentative basis: standardized meta-analysis where data permitted, supplemented by unstandardized effect comparisons and subgroup analysis.

Relevant results:

- The aggregate interpretation was a small positive effect on external quality and little or no discernible overall productivity effect.
- Industrial and academic subgroups differed: unstandardized industrial studies suggested larger quality gains and larger productivity reductions.
- Greater differences in testing effort were associated with larger productivity reductions; quality moderation varied by setting and available data.
- Individual primary studies reported positive, negative, and neutral outcomes.

Limitations stated or identified:

- Primary studies differed substantially in tasks, controls, participants, and outcome metrics.
- Standard deviations and other data needed for standardized effects were often missing.
- Unstandardized comparisons combine different scales and may ignore within-group variation.
- Industrial standardized evidence was especially sparse.
- Publication bias could not be evaluated adequately.
- Evidence current only through February 2011.

Reviewer appraisal:

- Relevance: moderate to high for a specific verification practice and its tradeoffs.
- Methodological fit: appropriate for synthesizing heterogeneous comparative TDD studies, but limited by primary-study reporting and construct inconsistency.
- Directness: direct for TDD relative to studied control practices; indirect for testing generally and for contemporary large systems.
- Precision: aggregate direction is available, but subgroup scarcity and heterogeneity weaken a universal effect estimate.
- Risk of bias: primary-study quality, missing-data handling, outcome heterogeneity, study dependence, and unknown publication bias.

Permitted use in synthesis: limited evidence that a disciplined test-first practice may improve external quality on average while its productivity consequences depend on context and test effort.

Dependency and joint-synthesis requirement: synthesize with ERB-01-01-S015. S014 concerns test-driven development as a specific technique; S015 qualifies inference from test presence and common unit-test measures. Neither source alone supports a general claim about all testing.

Prohibited inference: do not claim TDD always improves quality, has no productivity cost, represents every form of testing, or independently distinguishes engineers from programmers.

Candidate claim: verification method and effort can affect both quality and productivity, so engineering responsibility includes selecting and evaluating a context-appropriate testing strategy rather than merely requiring that tests exist.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending.

## ERB-01-01-S015

Source: Fabiano Pecorelli, Fabio Palomba, and Andrea De Lucia, “The Relation of Test-Related Factors to Software Quality: A Case Study on Apache Systems,” *Empirical Software Engineering*, 2021.

Stable location: [author-hosted full paper](https://fpalomba.github.io/pdf/Journals/J35.pdf)

Source role and type: multivocal factor discovery followed by an observational repository and test-analysis case study.

Population, system, and setting: production and unit-test classes from eight Apache Commons systems satisfying the study's build, language, repository, and issue-tracking criteria.

Responsibility examined: maintaining executable unit tests and test-suite characteristics including static metrics, coverage, mutation coverage, and test smells.

Outcome examined: production-class post-release defect counts.

Method or argumentative basis: statistical models evaluate test-related variables alone and with production-code and process confounders. The authors provide a public replication package and manually validate a sample of defect-linking results.

Relevant results:

- Test presence and executability were not statistically significant explanations of post-release defects after confounding factors were included.
- Finer-grained test factors had limited direct explanatory value; coverage-related properties were at most marginal in the reported models.
- Production size and pre-release change activity were more informative than most test-related measures.
- The authors distinguish a test suite's ability to detect current faults from its ability to predict future defect proneness.

Limitations stated or identified:

- The eight systems belong to one Apache project family and are Java/Maven projects.
- The analysis concerns unit-test-related measures, not integration, system, acceptance, exploratory, or operational testing.
- Test-to-code traceability and defect-linking methods can misclassify relationships.
- Repository metrics do not capture test intent, oracle quality, review quality, or all human testing work.
- Observational models do not establish causal effects.

Reviewer appraisal:

- Relevance: high as a challenge to simplistic testing metrics; moderate for testing responsibility as a whole.
- Methodological fit: suitable for testing whether common repository measures add explanatory power; unsuitable for concluding that testing is ineffective.
- Execution and reporting: selection, variables, confounders, models, validation, threats, and replication material are documented.
- Directness: direct for the measured Apache unit-test factors; indirect for other verification activities and settings.
- Risk of bias: system selection, construct validity, traceability, defect labeling, model specification, and residual confounding.

Permitted use in synthesis: qualify claims based on test existence or coverage alone and support the distinction between performing a nominal practice and exercising effective verification responsibility.

Dependency and joint-synthesis requirement: synthesize with ERB-01-01-S014. S015 does not measure all verification activity or the comparative effects of test-driven development; S014 does not establish that nominal test presence or coverage demonstrates effectiveness.

Prohibited inference: do not conclude that tests have no value, that coverage never matters, or that the study measures the defect-detection benefit of all testing activities.

Candidate claim: the presence or executability of tests is not sufficient evidence of delivered quality; verification responsibility also requires attention to test purpose, effectiveness, context, and outcome evaluation.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending.

## ERB-01-01-S010

Source: Shane McIntosh, Yasutaka Kamei, Bram Adams, and Ahmed E. Hassan, “An Empirical Study of the Impact of Modern Code Review Practices on Software Quality,” *Empirical Software Engineering*, 21(5), 2016, pp. 2146–2189.

Stable location: [author-hosted full paper](https://rebels.cs.uwaterloo.ca/papers/emse2016_mcintosh.pdf)

Source role and type: observational repository-mining case study using regression models.

Population, system, and setting: four releases from three large open-source systems using Gerrit: Qt, VTK, and ITK.

Responsibility examined: third-party review of proposed code changes, including review coverage, participation or discussion, and subject-matter expertise.

Outcome examined: component-level post-release defect counts during the six months after release, used as a software-quality proxy.

Method or argumentative basis: links version-control changes, review records, and defect fixes; models review measures while controlling for product, process, and authorship measures associated with defect proneness.

Relevant results:

- Review coverage added significant explanatory power in two of four release models, not all four.
- Discussion-related participation measures added explanatory power across the studied releases.
- Expertise-related review measures added explanatory power in the larger Qt releases.
- Components with complete review coverage could still contain post-release defects; coverage alone was insufficient.

Limitations stated or identified:

- Observational association does not establish causation.
- Defect-linking and review metrics are repository-derived proxies with possible classification error.
- Only three open-source systems and four releases were modeled.
- Unmeasured technical or organizational confounding may explain part of the relationship.
- Results differed by release and metric.

Reviewer appraisal:

- Relevance: high for whether review responsibility relates to a software outcome.
- Methodological fit: appropriate for estimating repository-level associations; inappropriate for a universal causal claim.
- Execution and reporting: systems, measures, controls, model construction, results, and threats are described; replication data were made available.
- Risk of bias: system selection, construct validity of proxies, researcher modeling decisions, and residual confounding.
- Directness: direct for modern review in the studied projects; indirect for other responsibilities and contexts.
- Precision: model-level estimates are reported, but cross-release inconsistency limits a single summary effect.

Permitted use in synthesis: limited evidence that the depth and expertise of review—not merely whether review occurred—were associated with post-release defect incidence in selected projects.

Prohibited inference: do not claim code review necessarily reduces defects, that review coverage guarantees quality, or that the association separates people called engineers from people called programmers.

Candidate claim: review responsibility may matter to post-release quality, but its execution, participation, expertise, and context appear more informative than a binary reviewed/not-reviewed label.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending. Must be synthesized jointly with ERB-01-01-S011.

## ERB-01-01-S012

Source: Daniel Méndez Fernández et al., “Naming the Pain in Requirements Engineering: Contemporary Problems, Causes, and Effects in Practice,” *Empirical Software Engineering*, 22(5), 2017, pp. 2298–2338, doi:10.1007/s10664-016-9451-7.

Stable location: [institutional author manuscript](https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/files/90711478/EMSE_D_15_00239_preprint.pdf)

Source role and type: multinational practitioner survey with qualitative coding and descriptive analysis.

Population, system, and setting: respondents representing 228 organizations across 10 countries, multiple domains, organization sizes, and agile, plan-driven, or mixed process settings. The authors describe the work as part of the replicated NaPiRE family of surveys.

Responsibility examined: requirements elicitation, analysis, specification, stakeholder communication, completeness, qualification and experience, customer access, time allocation, and change management.

Outcome examined: practitioner-reported project failure relevance and perceived effects on projects, organizations, design or implementation, and products.

Method or argumentative basis: respondents ranked 21 predefined requirements problems and supplied open-text accounts of causes and effects. Researchers coded qualitative responses using iterative and axial coding, with researcher triangulation.

Relevant results:

- Incomplete or hidden requirements were selected among the five most critical problems by 109 of 228 respondents; 43 of those selections identified the problem as a cause of project failure.
- Communication flaws between the project team and customer were selected by 93 respondents; 45 linked them to project failure.
- Moving targets were selected by 76 respondents; 39 linked them to project failure.
- For incomplete or hidden requirements, reported effects included time overrun, post-implementation rework, and poor product quality; broader effect categories included project or organization, design or implementation, and product consequences.
- Problem rankings and reported causes varied across organizational size and process context, while incomplete or hidden requirements remained prominent across the reported clusters.

Limitations stated or identified:

- The evidence captures respondent perceptions, not independently verified causes or outcomes.
- Representativeness cannot be estimated and domains are unevenly sampled.
- Safety-critical and other specialized contexts are underrepresented.
- Closed and predefined questionnaire elements may simplify project realities or prime responses.
- Respondents may apply process labels inconsistently.
- Manual qualitative coding involves researcher judgment despite triangulation.
- The paper's probabilistic cause-effect diagrams display relative citation frequencies, not causal probabilities.

Reviewer appraisal:

- Relevance: high for problem framing and requirements responsibilities before programming.
- Methodological fit: appropriate for identifying recurring practitioner-perceived problems and consequences; inappropriate for causal effect estimation.
- Execution and reporting: study design, population, coding, contextual blocks, results, validity controls, and limitations are extensively reported.
- Risk of bias: convenience and contact-network sampling, perception and recall bias, questionnaire framing, coding judgment, and uneven context coverage.
- Directness: direct for what participating organizations reported; indirect for actual failure mechanisms and objective outcome magnitude.
- Precision or adequacy: exact descriptive counts are available, but no population prevalence or causal effect can be estimated.
- Independence and conflicts: distributed academic research team; no evaluated commercial intervention identified.

Permitted use in synthesis: show that requirements completeness, change, and communication were repeatedly perceived as consequential across varied organizations; identify candidate mechanisms and downstream effects for stronger studies.

Prohibited inference: do not state that the study proves requirements problems caused project failure, convert survey frequencies into universal failure rates, or claim that one prescribed requirements process prevents the reported effects.

Candidate claims:

- In the surveyed organizations, important work and risk existed before code construction: clarifying needs, exposing hidden requirements, maintaining customer communication, and managing change.
- Participants connected failures in those responsibilities with rework, schedule, implementation, and product consequences, but the causal strength of those connections remains unestablished.
- Requirements responsibility is context-sensitive and cannot be reduced to producing a document or following one universal process.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending.

## ERB-01-01-S011

Source: Andrey Krutauz, Tapajit Dey, Peter C. Rigby, and Audris Mockus, “Do Code Review Measures Explain the Incidence of Post-Release Defects? Case Study Replications and Bayesian Networks,” arXiv:2005.09217, 2020.

Stable location: [full paper and replication study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.09217)

Source role and type: reproduction and external replication of S010 with alternative statistical modeling.

Population, system, and setting: original Qt data plus six releases of Google Chrome; repository, review-system, and issue-tracker records.

Responsibility examined: modern code-review coverage, discussion, participation, and expertise measures.

Outcome examined: component-level post-release defect incidence.

Method or argumentative basis: reproduces additive-regression analysis, tests it on Chrome, examines variable-selection sensitivity, and applies bootstrapped Bayesian-network models. Data and scripts are reported as publicly available.

Relevant results:

- Estimated review-measure influence was unstable across releases and sensitive to variable selection.
- Models excluding review predictors fit as well as or better than models containing them.
- Prior defects, component size, churn, and authorship showed more reproducible relationships with post-release defects.
- Bayesian-network models generally did not place review measures in a direct relationship with defects; some possible indirect relationships remained.

Limitations stated or identified:

- Repository measures imperfectly represent the quality and substance of human review.
- Operationalization differed where Chrome data did not exactly match the original study.
- Bayesian-network results depend on discretization, structure-search choices, and confidence thresholds.
- Learned network structure is not equivalent to demonstrated causal structure.
- The evidence still concerns a small number of large projects.

Reviewer appraisal:

- Relevance: very high because it directly tests a candidate outcome claim and the stability of S010.
- Methodological fit: strong for replication and sensitivity analysis; still observational for causal inference.
- Execution and reporting: data sources, reproduction, alternative models, sensitivity checks, and replication package are documented.
- Risk of bias: measurement validity, project selection, modeling discretion, and remaining confounding.
- Directness: direct for stability of review-metric associations in Qt and Chrome; indirect for the intrinsic value of review work that repository metrics do not capture.
- Precision: provides model-specific results rather than a stable general effect.

Permitted use in synthesis: materially lower confidence in any broad claim that repository measures of code review directly explain post-release defects; support the need to examine mechanisms, context, and alternative explanations.

Prohibited inference: do not conclude that review has no value. The study challenges particular measures and defect models, while acknowledging other outcomes such as problem solving and knowledge sharing.

Candidate claim: available repository evidence does not yet support a stable general causal claim that more measured review activity produces fewer post-release defects.

Review state: extracted by one researcher; independent review pending. Must be synthesized jointly with ERB-01-01-S010.

## ERB-01-01-S006

Source: Andre N. Meyer, Laura E. Barton, Gail C. Murphy, Thomas Zimmermann, and Thomas Fritz, “The Work Life of Developers: Activities, Switches and Perceived Productivity,” *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering*, 43(12), 2017, doi:10.1109/TSE.2017.2656886.

Stable location: [accessible full paper](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/2017-meyer.pdf)

Source role and type: in-situ field study combining background computer monitoring with experience-sampling self-reports.

Publication and observation date: published in 2017; study dates are not sufficiently identified in the inspected text for temporal reconstruction.

Population, system, and setting: 20 analyzed professional software developers from four companies in the United States, Canada, and Switzerland. Company size, project stage, product, language, and participant experience varied. Nineteen participants were men and one was a woman.

Activities and responsibilities identified: coding, debugging, review, version control, planning, documentation, meetings, email, work-related information seeking, and task coordination.

Level of analysis: individual work activity within organizational settings.

Outcome or consequence examined: activity distribution, activity switching, and self-perceived productivity; no direct measure of software quality, project success, safety, or reliability.

Method or argumentative basis: active-process and window-title capture every 10 seconds, input-event counts, and periodic activity and productivity self-reports over several weeks. Four of 24 recruited participants were removed for insufficient data.

Relevant results:

- In this sample, monitored work included substantial non-coding activity. Coding accounted for 21.0% of measured time, while identified collaborative activity accounted for 24.4%.
- Participants regularly moved among coding, work-related browsing, documentation, planning, communication, and other activities.
- Activity duration and composition varied materially across participants and companies.
- The study found no common explanatory model of perceived productivity across all participants.

Limitations stated or identified:

- Monitoring could not fully classify remote-desktop use or work away from the computer.
- Meeting time may be underestimated when it was not self-reported.
- Application activity is an imperfect proxy for responsibility, intent, and task boundaries.
- The small, gender-skewed convenience sample limits generalization.
- Perceived productivity is subjective and must not be substituted for engineering outcomes.

Reviewer appraisal:

- Relevance: high for observable work composition; low for direct comparison of programmer and engineer categories.
- Methodological fit: strong for fine-grained computer activity and switching; weaker for off-computer responsibility and outcomes.
- Execution and reporting: instrumentation, sampling, classification, and threats are described in detail.
- Risk of bias: recruitment through contacts, participant reactivity, classification judgment, missing activity, and small sample.
- Directness: direct for the studied developers and observed computer activity; indirect for the chapter's occupational boundary and outcome questions.
- Precision or adequacy: precise logging but limited semantic interpretation and population precision.
- Independence and conflicts: academic and industry-affiliated authors; no relevant commercial intervention evaluated.

## ERB-01-01-S007

Source: Andre N. Meyer, Earl T. Barr, Christian Bird, and Thomas Zimmermann, “Today Was a Good Day: The Daily Life of Software Developers,” *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering*, accepted 2019.

Stable location: [Microsoft Research preprint](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/devtime-preprint-TSE19.pdf)

Source role and type: large cross-sectional organizational survey with preliminary interviews and mixed qualitative and quantitative analysis.

Publication and observation date: manuscript accepted 2019; responses collected over four months.

Population, system, and setting: 5,971 anonymous prior-workday responses from professional developers working in the greater Seattle area at Microsoft. The organization contained varied teams, products, technologies, processes, and project stages.

Activities and responsibilities identified: reading and writing code and tests, debugging, testing, specification and requirements, code review, documentation, meetings, email, interruptions, helping or mentoring, relationship maintenance, learning, administration, and infrastructure work.

Level of analysis: individual workday within one large organization.

Outcome or consequence examined: whether respondents perceived the prior workday as good and typical; no direct product-quality or project-performance outcome.

Method or argumentative basis: 10 preliminary interviews used to validate an activity taxonomy, followed by an organization-wide survey asking respondents to reconstruct the previous workday and evaluate it. Open responses were coded and activity times were analyzed quantitatively.

Relevant results:

- Across 5,928 usable activity-time responses, reading or writing code and tests represented 15% of reported time. Bug fixing represented 14%, testing 8%, specification 4%, review 5%, and documentation 2%.
- Reported collaboration included meetings at 15%, email at 10%, helping or mentoring at 5%, interruptions at 4%, and networking at 2%.
- Respondents' assessment of meetings and interruptions depended on work phase and purpose; collaborative activity was not uniformly perceived as harmful.
- The study distinguishes individual coding progress from contributions that support team progress and warns that developer self-evaluation may undervalue collaborative work.

- All respondents worked for one large organization in one geographic region.
- Activity duration and workday quality were self-reported retrospectively.
- Anonymous sampling permits an unknown amount of repeat participation, although identified repeat responses were uncommon.

- Relevance: high for the breadth of professional developer activity and perceived consequences; low for a title-based engineer/programmer comparison.
- Methodological fit: suitable for a broad organizational snapshot and participant meaning; unsuitable for causal claims.
- Execution and reporting: survey development, sampling, coding, quantitative analysis, and threats are reported.
- Risk of bias: single-company sampling, self-report, possible repeated respondents, and organizational culture.
- Directness: direct for reported Microsoft developer workdays; indirect for other organizations, occupational categories, and objective outcomes.
- Precision or adequacy: large response count provides stable descriptive estimates within the setting, but the number of unique respondents is unknown.
- Independence and conflicts: two authors were affiliated with Microsoft Research and the studied population was Microsoft; this matters for transfer and interpretation, not automatic exclusion.

Prohibited inference: do not generalize percentages to all software workers, infer causality, treat perceived workday quality as system quality, or use the study to assign a programmer or engineer identity to respondents.

Candidate claims:

- In this large single-organization sample, professional development work was not dominated by writing code alone.
- Specification, testing, review, documentation, communication, and support were ordinary reported components of work.

## ERB-01-01-S005

Source: Brian Randell, “Fifty Years of Software Engineering — or — The View from Garmisch,” ICSE, May 2018.

Stable location: [full paper at arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.02742)

Source role and type: participant retrospective and conceptual/historical argument.

Population, system, and setting: the 1968–69 NATO conferences and later software-development contexts, especially large bespoke systems and mass-market package software.

Relevant content inspected: complete nine-page paper.

Definition and scope extracted:

- Randell reports that “software engineering” was chosen in 1968 as a provocative statement of a needed capability, rather than a description of a mature existing discipline.
- The paper examines a proposed distinction based on multi-person development and software that exists in multiple versions.
- It also presents a lifecycle-wide definition, from specification through post-deployment maintenance, while showing that the field's boundaries remain contested.

Activities and responsibilities identified: specification, production, maintenance, coordination across people and versions, dependability, cost, schedule, validation, socio-technical concerns, security, privacy, and adaptation to development context.

Level of analysis: historical field, project, organization, and software-market context.

Method or argumentative basis: firsthand retrospective combined with quotations, earlier publications, and selected examples; no systematic sampling or comparative causal design.

- Methodological fit: appropriate for history and conceptual interpretation; weak for prevalence and causal outcome claims.
- Transparency: sources and the author's role are stated; selection is narrative and may reflect memory and author judgment.
- Risk of bias: retrospective recall, selective examples, and author involvement in the original conference.
- Directness: direct for the author's interpretation and reported history; indirect for present-day practice.
- Recency: published in 2018, reflecting on events beginning in 1968.
