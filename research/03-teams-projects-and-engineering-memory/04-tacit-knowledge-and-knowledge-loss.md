## Primary research question

> Which engineering knowledge remains tacit, how is it transferred, and what happens when it becomes unavailable?

## Findings

### Tacit knowledge includes discrimination and context

Engineers may know which signal matters, whom to ask, which invariant is fragile, or why an apparent option failed without being able to recover it from formal artifacts.

### Teams maintain a map of who knows what

Transactive memory can route questions efficiently, but it creates dependence on availability and accurate expertise attribution. A named owner is not proof that knowledge is transferable.

### Transfer requires joint action and external traces

Conversation, pairing, review, observation, and guided tasks can transfer situated knowledge; tests, decisions, runbooks, examples, and histories preserve inspectable fragments. Documentation alone cannot encode every skill or context.

### Loss appears as uncertainty and delayed error

Personnel departure, stale attribution, fragmented records, or inaccessible communication increases search, repeated decisions, unsafe assumptions, and inability to explain behavior. The magnitude varies and cannot be inferred from headcount alone.

## Consequence for later evaluation

Evaluation must examine the actual dependency, information flow, decision, responsibility, artifact use, or outcome relevant to the claim. Message counts, approvals, documents, contributor counts, ticket closure, and process labels are proxies; none independently demonstrates collective capability or durable learning.

## Limitations

This is a structured, non-exhaustive synthesis across heterogeneous methods and settings. Several studies are observational, self-reported, historical, or organization-specific. The chapter supports bounded mechanisms and evaluation requirements, not a universal team design or process.

## Research boundary

This chapter studies software engineering generally. It does not inspect Zelyq, define product behavior, or authorize implementation.
