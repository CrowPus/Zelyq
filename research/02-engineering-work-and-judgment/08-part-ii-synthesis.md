## Synthesis

Reliable software-engineering judgment is not a personality trait and is not demonstrated by producing code quickly. It is task- and context-specific performance that keeps the problem, evidence, alternatives, uncertainty, action, and consequences connected.

Engineers move among activities with different purposes: discovering needs, acquiring system understanding, designing, constructing, verifying, coordinating, releasing, observing, and revising. Reliable work selects evidence appropriate to the activity. A test can constrain a behavioral claim; it cannot show that the right stakeholder problem was chosen. A design record can expose an alternative; it cannot prove the resulting system works in operation.

Judgment is visible when an engineer:

- states the task, boundary, desired outcome, constraints, and affected parties;
- distinguishes observation from inference and unknown from assumed;
- builds a task-relevant model and tests its predictions against the system;
- develops competing explanations or alternatives when consequence and uncertainty warrant them;
- seeks evidence capable of disconfirming the preferred account;
- communicates rationale, residual uncertainty, ownership, and escalation conditions;
- verifies both the immediate action and its wider consequence boundary; and
- updates the decision when feedback changes its evidential basis.

These practices are neither a mandatory linear sequence nor proof of success. Time pressure, missing access, collective knowledge, organizational authority, and domain criticality affect what one person can do. Reliable outcomes often depend on team and organizational conditions examined in Part III.

## Experience boundary

Experience supplies patterns, vocabulary, and faster recognition, but years and title combine unequal opportunities and feedback. Transfer is conditional on similarity of domain, task, system, tools, organization, and consequence. Part II therefore treats demonstrated discrimination, calibration, adaptation, and outcome evidence as stronger than tenure alone.

## Requirements carried forward

Later AI and trust chapters must evaluate more than final output. They need evidence of context acquisition, problem framing, causal discrimination, alternative consideration, uncertainty communication, verification, recovery, and calibration under defined conditions. They must also distinguish capabilities of an individual actor from capabilities created through coordination and organizational memory.

## Limitations and exit decision

The evidence base is heterogeneous and underrepresents safety-critical, small, non-Western, accessibility-focused, and adversarial work. Several findings are Moderate–Low because field realism and causal identification trade off. Within those limits, the part meets its exit criterion: it identifies observable practices and conditions associated with engineering judgment without equating expertise with status or tenure.

This synthesis is research, not a Zelyq requirement or code authorization.
