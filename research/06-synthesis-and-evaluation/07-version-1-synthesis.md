# The Engineering Research Book

## Version 1 synthesis — Evaluating an AI software engineering partner

Version: 1.0 research synthesis

Status: Reviewed with limitations

Evidence observation cutoff for changeable AI claims: 2026-08-25

Last substantive review: 2026-08-25

Traceability: [Version 1 traceability matrix](../TRACEABILITY_MATRIX.md)

## Governing answer

An AI system earns justified reliance as a software-engineering partner only for responsibilities, configurations, contexts, and consequence levels where current evidence demonstrates adequate capability, bounded failure, authorization discipline, inspectability, meaningful human control, and recovery—and where accountable people and institutions continue to validate those conditions.

No reviewed evidence supports a single binary conclusion that an AI “is an engineer” or is trustworthy in general. Partnership is an allocation of responsibilities within a sociotechnical system. It must be evaluated responsibility by responsibility and can be authorized, constrained, monitored, or withdrawn accordingly.

## What must be understood

Software engineering is continuing responsibility for a changing sociotechnical system. It requires more than producing source code: purpose and stakeholder framing, system understanding, uncertainty and tradeoff management, causal diagnosis, design, construction, verification, coordination, memory, operation, recovery, and organizational learning all matter.

Understanding is task-relative and distributed across code, runtime behavior, tests, documents, history, dependencies, people, and environment. Any AI evaluation that supplies a perfectly framed issue and checks only a patch samples a meaningful but narrow portion of engineering work.

## What current evidence demonstrates

At the August 25, 2026 cutoff, configured AI systems demonstrate bounded capabilities in repository search and question answering, code generation and modification, some real-issue resolution, tool interaction, constraint-rich planning, memory retrieval, critique, and multi-step technical tasks. Results depend materially on model snapshot, harness, context, tools, permissions, memory, verifier, environment, task distribution, attempts, and date.

The evidence also demonstrates material limitations: context-selection failure, long-context use problems, long-horizon drift, constraint loss, unsafe obedience to untrusted content, stale or confused memory, weak intrinsic verification, plausible semantic and security errors, non-determinism, correlated failures, and benchmark defects or contamination.

These findings support bounded assistance and carefully tested delegation. They do not establish autonomous end-to-end lifecycle responsibility or field reliability across software engineering.

## What justified reliance requires

For a defined responsibility, evaluation must establish:

1. the legitimate purpose, boundary, affected parties, consequence, and accountable owner;
2. the exact model–system configuration and evidence observation date;
3. direct performance evidence across relevant normal, edge, changed, and adversarial conditions;
4. failure, abstention, calibration, cost, latency, and recovery distributions—not only average completion;
5. context provenance, uncertainty discipline, and evidence capable of falsifying the system’s preferred account;
6. least-privilege authorization, data minimization, isolation, audit, and enforcement outside model instructions;
7. human oversight with information, competence, time, authority, and real ability to intervene;
8. containment, restoration or compensation, escalation, incident response, and affected-party redress;
9. continuing monitoring, expiry, revalidation, and withdrawal triggers; and
10. evidence that the human–AI–organization arrangement improves the intended outcome without hiding workload, knowledge loss, or responsibility.

## How to evaluate it

Use a portfolio of lifecycle scenarios rather than one aggregate benchmark. Include unfamiliar-system diagnosis, ambiguous requirements, cross-component change, adversarial tool use, memory update and deletion, review and dissent, operational recovery, and justified non-action. Map each scenario to responsibility-specific probes and independent failure criteria.

Report results at the configured-system level with repetitions, uncertainty, conditional slices, severity, correlated failure, cost, and date. Validate benchmark tasks and oracles. Separate possibility, repeatability, robustness, field reliability, and trustworthy conduct.

## What Version 1 cannot defend

Version 1 cannot defend claims that:

- code-generation ability equals software-engineering ability;
- repository access equals system understanding;
- a model’s general benchmark rank predicts a project outcome;
- explanations or confidence displays necessarily improve reliance;
- a nominal human reviewer guarantees oversight;
- multiple models or reviewers fail independently;
- tests alone establish requirements, security, maintainability, or operational success;
- memory should retain everything;
- collaboration automatically produces synergy; or
- responsibility can be assigned to the AI system itself.

## Research and engineering boundary

This book supplies a research-backed decision framework and evidence requirements. It deliberately does not specify Zelyq’s features, architecture, models, permissions, workflows, or code. A separate engineering agent must first verify project context, then create an engineering application entry that cites applicable reviewed findings, records assumptions and alternatives, defines authorization and evaluation, and receives the required approval before implementation.

## Final limitation

The book is a Version 1 evidence synthesis, not a claim of completeness or permanent truth. Its sources underrepresent many regions, languages, organizations, critical domains, accessibility needs, long-term deployments, and unpublished failures. AI evidence is especially perishable. The correct response is continued review and bounded reliance—not false certainty.
