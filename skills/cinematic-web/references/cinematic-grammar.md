# Cinematic Grammar

## Purpose

Give the agent a vocabulary for designing motion with intent. This is a design framework, not a rigid law.

## Start with the story

Before motion, identify:
- **subject** — what the viewer should care about;
- **promise** — what the experience communicates;
- **sequence** — what must be understood first, second, and last;
- **payoff** — the moment the experience resolves into meaning or action.

A premium page usually needs fewer, stronger beats rather than continuous spectacle.

## Scene purposes

### Establish
Introduce the world, product, or premise. Favor stable readable composition over immediate complexity.

### Reveal
Move from concealment to understanding.

### Focus
Remove competing information and direct attention to one detail.

### Travel
Move the viewer through depth or around a subject. Travel should reveal information.

### Transform
Change object, state, material, environment, or layout to explain a concept.

### Compare
Show before/after, inside/outside, old/new, or two modes.

### Escalate
Increase energy, density, depth, speed, or scale before a climax.

### Resolve
Reduce visual complexity after the climax.

### Convert
Present CTA, price, next step, or primary decision after the narrative earns attention.

## Rhythm

Cinematic rhythm comes from contrast:
- motion / stillness;
- dense / sparse;
- near / far;
- bright / dark;
- fast / slow;
- reveal / hold.

Do not animate every section at the same speed or intensity.

A useful sequence is:

`establish → reveal → focus → escalate → resolve → convert`

but content determines the order.

## Holds matter

Scroll-linked work often fails because every pixel changes something. Add visual holds where the camera or subject changes very little while the viewer reads.

## Transition hierarchy

Prefer:
1. composition change;
2. camera or subject motion;
3. material/environment change;
4. effects.

Do not use effects to hide weak composition.

## Restraint test

For every effect ask:
- What information does this reveal?
- What emotion does this support?
- What transition problem does this solve?
- Would the page be stronger without it?

If no answer survives, remove it.

## Storyboard fields

For each scene record:
- purpose;
- dominant subject;
- copy;
- entry state;
- exit state;
- camera action;
- subject action;
- hold/read time;
- mobile behavior;
- reduced-motion behavior.

## Common failures

- **Continuous spectacle** — everything moves, so nothing feels important.
- **No hierarchy** — headline, model, particles, and camera compete.
- **No resolution** — the experience never calms enough to deliver the message.
- **Desktop-only composition** — portrait screens collapse.
- **Scroll length without content** — long pinning with little visible change.
